01 · Vine Training & Canopy Care

Walk a vineyard row and see how people shape a vine to manage sun, air, and water.

flowchart LR
  A["Arrive at the row"] -->|"look at the trunk"| B["Spot the vine's frame"]
  B -->|"is it tall or low?"| C["Head-trained vs trellised"]
  C -->|"follow the wires"| D["Cane vs spur pruning"]
  D -->|"step into the leaves"| E["Canopy: shade or sun?"]
  E -->|"feel the airflow"| F["Disease and moisture risk"]
  F -->|"check the soil"| G["Water: rain, dry-farm, or drip"]
  G -->|"ask the grower"| H["Why this site chose this shape"]
  H -->|"taste the result"| I["Link canopy to ripeness in the glass"]
  I --> J["Give-Back: water stewardship in the vineyard"]

What you practice

  • Naming two or three common vine shapes by sight
  • Explaining why leaf thinning or keeping shade changes ripeness
  • Connecting irrigation choices to climate and water scarcity
  • Asking a grower one good "why" question

Source vs Flow

*Source* — recognize a trained vs untrained vine and say what a canopy does. *Flow* — connect a site's water and sun to its training choice and predict the style effect.

Bridge: Hops climb trellises too — barley and grain fields and hop yards face the same sun, air, and water trade-offs. Agave and grain for spirits are farmed crops with their own water footprint.

Give-Back: Every vineyard decision is a water decision. Learners note one water-saving practice and how a similar idea could help a community well or garden.

02 · White Grape Varieties — Species Analysis

Original Sip Studies teaching. Public variety names. Not affiliated with any court, guild, or credentialing body.

Analyze the core white wine grapes as plants and as styles: who each variety is, where it grows best, how its structure reads, and how to explain it to a guest.

Sip Families (white): Crisp = high acid, light body, little or no oak · Floral = aromatic, perfumed, often lower acid or with sweetness · Rich = fuller body, often oak, ripeness, or lees texture. A variety can move between families depending on climate and winemaking (shown with →).

What you practice

  • Run the nine-step analysis on one variety and fill one table row from memory
  • Explain how climate moves a grape between Crisp and Rich (Chardonnay, Chenin Blanc)
  • Name a variety's home region and one place it travelled to
  • Say one guest-ready sentence per variety

Source vs Flow

Source: name each variety, place it in its Sip Family, and give its climate fit and one home region. Flow: compare two varieties step by step (for example Riesling vs Gewürztraminer), predict style from climate and winemaking, and recommend one to a guest with a reason.

Bridge: Grape spirits (brandies, pomace spirits) start from white grapes like Ugni Blanc, Folle Blanche, and Muscat relatives: same fruit, a different house.

Give-Back: Each variety row ends with a water note. Learners pick one white and write its water story in two sentences: how the vine gets its water and one way a grower saves it. Somm Support uses these stories at Water to Wine events.

03 · Red Grape Varieties — Species Analysis

Original Sip Studies teaching. Public variety names. Not affiliated with any court, guild, or credentialing body.

Analyze the core red wine grapes as plants and as styles: skin, ripening, structure, place, and how to explain each one to a guest.

Sip Families (red): Light = pale, high acid, low grip · Medium = balanced fruit, acid, and grip; often savory · Bold = deep color or high tannin, full body, often oak. Skin thickness and climate decide most of the move between families (shown with →).

What you practice

  • Run the nine-step analysis on one variety and fill one table row from memory
  • Link skin thickness and ripening time to color and grip
  • Explain synonym pairs (Syrah/Shiraz, Grenache/Garnacha, Zinfandel/Primitivo, Mourvèdre/Monastrell)
  • Say one guest-ready sentence per variety

Source vs Flow

Source: name each variety, place it in its Sip Family, and give its climate fit and one home region. Flow: compare two varieties step by step (for example Nebbiolo vs Pinot Noir: both pale, very different grip), predict style from site and winemaking, and guide a guest from one family to another.

Bridge: Bold, oak-aged reds and barrel-aged spirits share vocabulary for grip and wood. Learners meet that bridge again in Chart 06.

Give-Back: Red grapes need long seasons and often irrigation. Learners pick one dry-farmed or water-wise red (old-vine Zinfandel, bush-vine Grenache) and share one fact a guest might remember. Each story can headline a Water to Wine pour.

Follow three crops — grapes, grain/hops, and spirit bases — from field to the making house door.

  A["Check the crop"] -->|"is it ready?"| B["Ripeness checks: taste, sugar, look"]
  B -->|"choose a lane"| C["Grapes"]
  B --> D["Grain and hops"]
  B --> E["Spirit bases: agave, cane, grain, fruit"]
  C -->|"pick by hand or machine"| F["Shears, bins, harvester"]
  D -->|"cut, dry, malt"| G["Combines, kilns, hop dryers"]
  E -->|"cut, roast, press"| H["Coa, ovens, mills"]

  G --> I
  H --> I
  I -->|"deliver"| J["Arrive at the making house"]

What you practice

  • Naming one ripeness signal per crop
  • Comparing hand harvest vs machine harvest trade-offs
  • Explaining why grain is malted and why agave is cooked
  • Tracking time-to-processing and why it matters

Source vs Flow

*Source* — name the crop and its main harvest tool. *Flow* — compare how harvest choices change the final drink across all three lanes.

Give-Back: Harvest and cleanup use large volumes of water. Learners list one reuse or capture idea and connect it to Somm Support's access projects.

05 · Making Houses: Cellar, Brew House, Still House

Tour three making houses and see how each turns sugar into drink.

flowchart TD
  A["Raw material arrives"] -->|"choose your house"| B["Cellar: wine"]
  A --> C["Brew House: beer"]
  A --> D["Still House: spirits"]
  B -->|"crush and press"| E["Ferment in tanks or barrels"]
  C -->|"mash, boil, add hops"| F["Ferment in fermenters"]
  D -->|"ferment a wash"| G["Distill in pot or column still"]
  E --> H["Shared step: yeast turns sugar to alcohol"]
  F --> H
  G -->|"cut heads, hearts, tails"| H
  H -->|"rest or age"| I["Decide: fresh or aged?"]
  I --> J["Ready for Chart 06 or 07"]
  J --> K["Give-Back: water in every recipe"]

What you practice

  • Naming the key vessel in each house: press, mash tun, still
  • Explaining fermentation as the shared heart of all three
  • Telling pot still from column still in one sentence
  • Mapping where water enters each process

Source vs Flow

*Source* — name each house's main step and equipment. *Flow* — compare how the same idea (sugar → alcohol) creates different drinks, and recommend which house a guest's favorite came from.

Give-Back: Beer is mostly water; spirits are cut with water; wineries clean with water. Learners pick one house and write its water-saving pledge.

Learn how time, wood, and local tradition shape style — using Sip Studies scaffolds, not rulebooks.

  C --> F["Country pattern cards"]

  B --> F

  F -->|"New-World pattern"| H["Producer chooses; label tells the story"]
  F -->|"Spirit pattern"| I["Age statements and blends"]
  F -->|"Beer pattern"| J["Barrel-aged and sour styles"]

  H --> K
  I --> K
  J --> K
  K --> L["Give-Back: patience and legacy"]

What you practice

  • Sorting a drink into Fresh, Rested, or Wood lanes
  • Explaining what a barrel adds
  • Using four Sip Studies pattern cards to read a label for aging clues
  • Explaining why some places set aging rules and others don't

Source vs Flow

*Source* — recognize whether a drink was aged and in what. *Flow* — compare two country patterns and recommend a fresh or aged option for a guest. (Learners look up current local rules from official sources; Sip Studies teaches the pattern, not the numbers.)

Give-Back: Aging is legacy — something made now for people later. Learners connect that to building water access that lasts beyond one season.

07 · Bottle to Shelf: Packaging & Distribution

Follow a finished drink from tank to the shop or restaurant door.

flowchart LR
  A["Finished drink in tank"] -->|"filter or not?"| B["Clarify and stabilize"]
  B -->|"choose a package"| C["Bottle, can, keg, or box"]
  C -->|"close it"| D["Cork, screwcap, crown, or keg coupler"]
  D -->|"label it"| E["Label and lot code"]
  E -->|"ship it"| F["Producer to importer or distributor"]
  F -->|"store it right"| G["Warehouse: cool, dark, rotated"]
  G -->|"deliver"| H["Retailer or restaurant"]
  H --> I["Track freshness: beer fast, wine and spirits slower"]
  I --> J["Give-Back: lighter packaging, less waste"]

What you practice

  • Matching package type to drink and use (can for beer, keg for draft, bottle for wine)
  • Explaining why closures matter
  • Drawing the producer → distributor → seller path in your area
  • Spotting freshness and storage risks

Source vs Flow

*Source* — name the package, closure, and main hand-offs. *Flow* — explain why a package was chosen and how distribution affects price and freshness for a guest.

Give-Back: Lighter glass, cans, and kegs cut shipping weight and water use. Learners propose one swap a local shop or bar could make.

Give-Back: Table & Tap starts with water — every guest is offered a glass first. Programs can tag one menu item as a "Water to Wine" pour that funds Somm Support access.
